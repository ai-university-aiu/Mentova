Each input of this task scatters ragged colored fragments across
the grid - up to ten pieces, some not even rectangular - plus a
three-cell L-tromino marker drawn in a color that appears nowhere
else. The marker is a corner bracket: its three cells fill a
two-by-two box except for one missing cell, and that cupped cell
is a triple command. It says WHERE the finished picture stands,
WHICH of its corners touches the spot, and WHICH piece never
moves - because the fragments are jigsaw pieces that tile, by
translation alone, a rectangle whose area equals their total cell
count, pinned by the piece that contains the cupped cell and
ringed by a uniform border in the color found right there.

The delicate part is that an airtight-looking exact-cover tiling
is still ambiguous. With only the uniform-border constraint the
solver finds TWO tilings of the 22x10 test rectangle - and
prefers the false one, whose color frames are torn into separated
clusters. The fix is the coherence law: every non-border color of
the finished picture must form exactly one connected component.
That single demand rejects the incoherent assembly and accepts
the true picture - three clean nested rings stacked in a column -
and it holds silently on both training pairs as well.

The wave's lesson: the human jigsaw instinct that a picture's
frames must not fall apart is a formal constraint, one connected
component per color, and it is exactly the constraint the puzzle
demands. Three cells of marker suffice to command position,
orientation, and anchor at once.
