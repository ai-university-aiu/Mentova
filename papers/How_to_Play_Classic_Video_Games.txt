HOW TO PLAY CLASSIC VIDEO GAMES

A Reference on How Humans Learn and Play Unfamiliar Games,
Written to Inform an Agent That Must Play Unseen Interactive Games With No Manual

Author: D. R. Dison, Founder of AIU, Creator and Owner of PrologAI and Mentova, ORCID 0009-0001-9246-5758, linkedin.com/in/d-r-dison/
Repository: https://github.com/ai-university-aiu/Mentova

Last updated: 2026-07-11.


PURPOSE

Mentova must play interactive games it has never seen, with no instructions, no
stated goal, and only a picture and a set of buttons. This is the same problem a
human faces when handed an unlabelled cartridge. Humans are remarkably good at
it. This document gathers how they do it - the conventions games share, the
mechanics players recognise on sight, the heuristics they use on a brand-new
game, and the efficiency insights of expert play - and ends with a concrete,
prioritised list of priors an unseen-game agent should follow. It is written to
be used as a reference by the solving machinery, not as an essay.

A note on scope. The games this informs are grid-based and arcade-flavoured:
avatars, keys, doors, hazards, timers, lives, score. The lessons are drawn from
the classic canon (arcade, Nintendo Entertainment System, and similar) because
those games are small, legible, and built to be understood without words.


PART ONE - GENRE CONVENTIONS AND THEIR CORE MECHANICS

Games fall into a handful of families, each with a defining loop, a win
condition, and a fail condition. Recognising the family early tells a player most
of what to do.

Maze-and-collect (for example Pac-Man). The avatar roams a maze, gathering every
pellet while avoiding roaming enemies; power-ups briefly reverse the threat. Win
by clearing the board; fail by contact with an enemy.

Platformer (for example Super Mario Bros.). The avatar moves generally rightward
across a stage, jumping over pits and onto or over enemies, to reach an exit
(a flag or door). Win by reaching the exit; fail by touching a hazard or falling.

Top-down adventure and block-puzzle (for example The Legend of Zelda, Sokoban,
Chip's Challenge). The avatar navigates a room, pushes blocks, collects keys, and
passes through doors to reach an exit. Win by reaching the exit or solving the
room; fail by hazard or by an unsolvable move.

Shooter (for example Space Invaders). The avatar fires at descending enemies and
dodges their fire. Win by clearing the wave; fail by being hit or overrun.

Lock-and-key and switch-and-door puzzles. A key found in the level opens a locked
door; a switch or pressure plate opens a gate. This is one of the oldest and most
universal conventions: levels make use of locked doors that can only be opened
with a specific key found elsewhere in the level.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game

Score-and-timer games. Many early arcade games are unbeatable by design and have
an indefinite number of levels; the only goal is to get as far as possible and
maximise score. Others end by finishing a sequence of levels toward a final goal.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game

The common spine across all of them: the player moves an avatar through a space,
as a progression of stages, encountering things that help progress (keys to open
doors) and things that end it (hazards).
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game


PART TWO - THE UNIVERSAL MECHANICS AND HOW THEY ARE RECOGNISED ON SIGHT

A small set of elements recurs across nearly every classic game. Players
recognise each by its behaviour, not its label.

The avatar. The player controls a character, usually a protagonist or avatar,
that navigates the level, collects objects, avoids obstacles, and fights enemies.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game
A human identifies the avatar in seconds by one test: it is the thing on screen
that moves in response to a button. Nothing else answers to the controller.

Health and lives. The avatar often has hit-points or health depleted by hazards,
and a small number of lives - chances to retry after death. Extra lives may be
earned by reaching a score threshold or finding an object.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game

Keys, doors, locks, switches, gates. A key opens a matching door; a switch opens
a gate. The player learns the pairing by seeing a door refuse to open, finding a
key, and watching the door yield.

Hazards and enemies. In any game that involves navigating a space, the player
meets obstacles, traps, and enemies; contact with them costs health or a life.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_game
A human marks a thing as a hazard the first time it hurts them, and thereafter
gives it a wide berth.

Collectibles, targets, and exits. Pellets, coins, keys, and the level exit are
the things to move toward. The exit or level-complete marker is the goal of a
stage.

Status displays. Timers, move-limits, lives, and score usually sit in a fixed
band (a status bar) separate from the play area. A player learns to read the
score going up as "that was good" and the timer going down as "hurry".


PART THREE - HOW A PLAYER APPROACHES A BRAND-NEW GAME (THE HEURISTICS)

This is the heart of the matter: what a person actually does in the first minute
of a game they have never seen and cannot read about.

Map the controls first. The player presses each button and watches what changes.
This is deliberate, systematic experimentation - try every input, note its
effect - and it is the first thing anyone does. It is the human version of a
control survey: which button moves me, which acts on the world, which does
nothing here.

Find the avatar. Among everything on screen, the player watches for the one
object that reacts to input. That object is "me". Everything else is either a
thing to reach or a thing to avoid.

Infer the goal by moving toward the obvious and watching for progress. With no
instructions, players set their own goal and monitor whether they are achieving
it: they execute strategies of goal-setting, monitoring of goal achievement, and
assessment of the strategies' effectiveness.
Source: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1090277.pdf
Concretely, they move the avatar toward the most salient, most "reachable-looking"
object - a door, a glowing item, an opening - and check whether something good
happened: the score rose, a door opened, the screen advanced. Progress is the
teacher.

Learn hazards from failure, in a low-stress way. Good games let players learn
from calculated failure: they introduce a hazard in a place where dying is cheap,
so the player learns what kills them and thereafter avoids it. Mega Man's design
is a masterclass in teaching through forcing functions and calculated player
failure, letting players learn from failure in low-stress environments and
encouraging experimentation.
Source: https://gameafternoon.com/blogs/design-inspiration/mega-man-x-how-to-tutorialize
The player's rule is simple: whatever just ended my run, do not do that again from
there.

Read design nudges. Games teach without words by design. Half-Life 2 uses
scripted events - such as showing how enemies behave - to nudge the player without
text. Nintendo strove to make games that taught as they were played.
Sources: https://lemmy.world/post/3777063 ; https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/nintendo-can-teach-us-game-design/

Recognise the "introduce, then combine" grammar. Super Mario Bros. World 1-1 is
the canonical example: within that one level the player is meant to understand
what the character is and what the game is about. Each new mechanic, hazard, and
enemy is introduced in isolation so the player can recognise it and learn its
behaviour; then mechanics are combined. World 1-1 uses only nine elements
(treasure blocks, breakable blocks, a Goomba, pits, pipes, platforms, a mushroom,
coins, a Piranha Plant), each taught before it is combined.
Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07721 ; https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/nintendo-can-teach-us-game-design/
For an agent, the lesson is that early levels are simple and legible: the first
level is the place to learn the game's atoms, because they appear alone there.

Transfer from other games. Players who have played many titles are faster on a new
one: they identify core mechanics, recognise patterns, and transfer skills between
games, adapting and recalibrating as they go.
Source: https://www.companionlink.com/blog/2026/02/discoverability-choice-and-learning-across-diverse-pc-games/
A key that looks like a key, a door that looks like a door, an avatar that moves
like an avatar - the priors carry over.

The Core Knowledge a human brings. Underneath all of this sits a small set of
innate priors the player does not have to learn: that the world is made of
persistent objects (Elizabeth Spelke's Core Knowledge theory calls these the
object, agent, number, and geometry systems), that some objects are agents that
act toward goals, that behaviour is goal-directed, that space can be navigated,
and that actions cause effects. These are exactly the priors the ARC benchmarks
assume and that an unseen-game agent must supply for itself.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Spelke ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.00289

The measured priority of the priors. It is not a matter of taste which prior
matters most; it has been measured. Dubey and colleagues (ICML 2018, Investigating
Human Priors for Playing Video Games) had one hundred and twenty people play the
same platformer under conditions that each removed one prior, and timed them.
Baseline players finished in about one and three-quarter minutes. Removing the
sense that visibly distinct things are objects worth investigating slowed them to
nearly eight minutes with six times the deaths - by far the largest effect.
Removing "things that look alike behave alike" was almost as damaging. Removing
the meaning of a sprite (that this shape is a ladder, that one a spike) roughly
doubled the time; removing what-can-be-interacted-with, gravity, and the control
mapping each cost less. Stripping all object priors at once collapsed play to
about twenty minutes and forty deaths. The order of importance, measured, is:
objectness first, then visual similarity, then the meaning of objects, then what
can be interacted with, then object-specific interactions, then physics, then the
control mapping. An agent should invest its prior-building effort in that order.
Source: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.10217

What a winning ARC-AGI-3 agent actually did with these priors. The training-free
graph-based agent that placed third on the private leaderboard reads almost
one-to-one off this list: it segments each frame into single-colour connected
components (objects), masks the status bars so a ticking score or timer does not
explode its memory, ranks candidate actions in salience tiers by object size,
shape, and colour prominence and acts on the most salient first, and keeps a graph
of states and transitions so it probes untested salient actions here or walks to
the nearest state with something untested. Objectness, salience-toward-target,
status-masking, and memory-backed systematic search - the human priors, made
mechanical.
Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2512.24156v1


PART FOUR - EFFICIENCY AND SPEEDRUNNING

Expert play is not just correct; it is efficient. The lessons transfer directly to
any agent scored on how few actions it uses.

Plan the route before executing it. Speedrun routing is deliberate: runners,
routers, and glitch-hunters iterate a route until it is optimised, planning the
path before running it.
Source: https://www.speedrun.com/forums/speedrunning/ls5x3

Take the shortest path to the goal. Route optimisation in games uses the same
pathfinding as anywhere else - shortest-path search such as A-star and Dijkstra's
algorithm - to move toward the objective with the fewest steps.
Source: https://yetiai.com/ai-in-speedrunning-optimizing-routes/

Do not waste moves. Every wasted input is time lost. Experts avoid detours,
avoid re-treading ground, and head straight for what advances the game. Where a
game imposes a move-limit or timer, this is not a preference but a requirement:
progress must be made before the allowance runs out.

Learn the map, then exploit it. The first pass explores; later passes exploit the
known route. This is the same carry-forward an agent gets from remembering the
states and paths it has already mapped.


PART FIVE - ACTIONABLE PRIORS FOR AN UNSEEN-GAME AGENT

This section distills the above into a concrete, prioritised policy for an agent
that must play an unfamiliar grid game with no instructions. It is ordered: earlier
priors take precedence over later ones. The ordering follows the measured
importance of priors (objectness first of all), and it matches what the winning
ARC-AGI-3 agents actually do.

0. Parse the frame into objects first, and remember them across frames. Segment
   the grid into connected same-colour regions and treat those, not raw cells, as
   the things to reason about. This is the highest-value prior of all - more
   important than any single heuristic below - because every other step reasons
   over objects: the avatar is an object, targets and hazards are objects, a key
   and its door are objects. Track each object's identity from one frame to the
   next so it is understood to persist.

1. Map the controls first. On a new game, try each available action at least once
   and record its effect, before repeating any. Breadth before depth. (Mentova
   already does this: the explore-novel choice tries each untried action first.)

2. Identify the avatar. The avatar is the object that moves in response to a move
   action - the small cluster of cells that shifts position after an action while
   the rest of the scene holds still. Track its cell. Everything else is a target
   or a hazard.

3. Learn the control-to-direction map. When an action moves the avatar from one
   cell to another, record the spatial displacement of that action (up, down,
   left, right). This lets the agent steer.

4. Move the avatar toward the nearest salient, non-hazard target. Once the avatar
   and a direction map are known, prefer the move that reduces the distance from
   the avatar to the nearest salient object that is not a known hazard - a door,
   an item, an opening. This is the universal classic-game loop, and the shortest-
   path instinct of expert play. Fall back to undirected exploration only when no
   such target or map is known yet.

5. Treat contact-death and game-ending states as hazards to avoid. Whatever ended
   the game from a state, do not repeat it from a like state. (Mentova records
   fatal moves; the open weakness is recognising the "like state" when a counter
   or animation changes the picture - a signature that ignores such changing
   regions is required.)

6. Recognise progress and lock onto it. When the score rises, a level completes,
   a door opens, or the screen advances, the action path that caused it is good:
   remember it, reinforce it, and reuse it. Progress is the only goal signal an
   instruction-free game gives, so it must be seized whenever it appears. For
   ARC-AGI-3 specifically, an increase in the reported levels-completed is exactly
   this signal.

7. Act under an assumed move budget. Assume the level must be completed within a
   limited number of actions and that wasted moves are lost progress. Prefer the
   move that advances the game; do not re-tread mapped ground; head for the
   obvious target rather than wandering. Where the game ends at a fixed step count
   without progress, the failure is not death but running out of allowance - the
   remedy is goal-directed progress, not more wandering.

8. The first level is the teacher. Early levels introduce the game's atoms in
   isolation and are the simplest and most legible. Spend the first level learning
   the avatar, the map, and what each object does; carry that forward.

9. Carry everything forward. What is learned on one attempt or level - the avatar,
   the direction map, the hazards, the progress paths, the map of states - must be
   kept and reused on the next. A later attempt should never re-learn from zero.

10. Mask the status region before remembering a state. Counters, timers, lives,
   and score sit in a fixed band and change constantly; if they are part of the
   picture-signature, every tick makes the same situation look like a brand-new
   state and the memory of where you have been fragments. Detect the cells that
   change on nearly every step regardless of the action - the status band and any
   animation - and exclude them from the state signature, while still reading them
   as reward and budget. This is the standing prerequisite that makes the state
   graph and the fatal-move memory actually carry forward, and the winning agent
   masks the status bars for exactly this reason.

11. Generalise labels by visual similarity. Once an object class is labelled a
   hazard, a collectible, or a wall by what happened on contact, apply that label
   to every object that looks alike - same colour and shape. Things that look
   alike behave alike is the second most valuable prior after objectness, so a
   single painful or rewarding contact should teach the whole class at once, not
   just the one object touched.

12. Encode the lock-and-key schema. If a barrier blocks the avatar and a distinct
   object of matching colour or shape sits elsewhere, hypothesise that the distant
   object is the enabler: go to it, then to the barrier, and test. Keys and doors,
   switches and gates, and "gather N items then the exit opens" are all this one
   template. If the barrier is seen before its key, hold the barrier as a pending
   goal.

These priors are the bridge between "explore an unknown environment" and "play a
game": exploration finds the pieces, but it is these game-shaped priors - an
avatar, a goal to move toward, hazards to avoid, progress to seize, and a budget
to respect - that turn wandering into play.
