TASK DESCRIPTION

Task b6f77b65 presents five training pairs and one test case, all
on 12x12 grids. The corner cell at row zero, column zero carries a
lone indicator color. The rest of the grid holds a standing
scaffold of colored pieces: vertical poles, horizontal bars, and
single wedged cells, interlocked so tightly that pieces of one
color can appear split into several fragments and pieces can hide
cells inside each other.

The indicator names the piece color to delete. When no piece
carries that color, the output is the unchanged input - the first
training pair demonstrates exactly this. Otherwise the named
pieces vanish and the survivors settle under gravity, each rigid
piece falling straight down until something holds it again.

Two readings of this task had failed before. The wave-90-era
survey called it a template dictionary; the Wave 105 survey
re-classified it as gravity with hidden geometry but left the
geometry unexplained. The key insight of this wave is that the
scene is a STATICS problem: the input is a stable scaffold in
which every piece already has a reason not to fall, and listing
those reasons for every piece of every training input is what
reveals the true mechanics. Bars hang pinched between two poles.
Poles stand impaled one cell deep inside the bars they rest on -
which is why the color-2 piece of the second training pair shows
seven cells in the input but eight in the output: its bottom cell
was hidden inside the bar all along. Single cells wedge between
two neighbors and drop free the moment one side disappears. A
piece whose visible cells appear to mirror vertically between
input and output dissolves into a pole plus a separate one-cell
piece jammed against its top.

RULE: staple_gravity

The glass-box rule is a six-stage procedure whose parameters are
all read off the input grid at solve time:

1. INDICATOR (arc2_cell_/4)
   The top-left corner cell names the color K of the pieces to
   delete. The corner cell itself belongs to no piece.

2. PIECE PARSING (sg_cells_/2, sg_comps_/2, sg_flood_/6,
   sg_decompose_/3, sg_vruns_/3, sg_vsplit_/2, sg_hsplit_/2,
   sg_weld_/4, sg_foot_/2)
   Every other foreground cell joins a same-color 4-connected
   component. Each component splits into rigid pieces: maximal
   vertical runs of at least two cells are poles; the leftover
   cells form maximal horizontal runs that stand as bars; a bar
   welds onto a pole as its foot only when one of its ends
   touches the pole's bottom cell. A bar beside a pole's top cell
   stays a separate piece - this is what separates a wedged
   single cell from a rigid arm.

3. IMPALEMENT (sg_impale_/2, sg_impale_one_/4)
   A pole whose bottom cell sits directly above a cell of a
   foreign bar extends one hidden cell into the bar. The two
   pieces share that cell; the bar is drawn in front.

4. DELETION
   Every piece carrying color K is removed. If none matches, the
   output is the unchanged input.

5. SETTLING (sg_settle_/4, sg_support_fix_/4, sg_supported_/5,
   sg_occ_/4, sg_pinched_/4, sg_segend_/4, sg_drop_/5)
   The supported pieces are computed as a least fixpoint of four
   conditions: a cell on the bottom grid row; a cell of a
   supported piece directly below one of the piece's cells; a
   cell shared with a supported piece; or a horizontal segment
   whose two outside flanks both belong to supported pieces.
   Because support grows as a fixpoint from the floor upward, a
   falling piece can support nothing - a bar cannot catch on a
   neighbor that is itself still mid-fall. Every unsupported
   piece then falls one row, and the ticks repeat until the
   whole scaffold is supported.

6. RENDERING (sg_render_/5)
   The settled pieces are painted on a fresh background - all
   vertical cells first, all horizontal cells after, so bars sit
   in front of the pole bottoms impaled inside them - and the
   corner indicator is restored.

The discriminating observation is the hidden impaled cell. The
second training input shows seven cells of the color-2 piece while
its settled output shows eight; only a pole that already owns a
cell inside the bar it stands on can grow a visible cell by
falling out of alignment with that bar. The same impalement powers
the corner case of the third training pair, where a wedged single
cell drops to the floor and the pole that rested on it follows,
landing with its own hidden bottom cell inside the fallen wedge.

VERIFICATION

The complete rule was written in plain English covering all five
training pairs and the test case before any code, following the
proven wave method: all twelve grids were dumped to text, every
piece of both distinct training inputs was catalogued with the
reason it does not fall, every fall distance in all four settling
pairs was derived by hand from the support rules, and the test
pair - whose scaffold survives its deletion fully supported, so
nothing moves - was confirmed cell by cell. A standalone Python
prototype confirmed all six grids on its first run before
implementation began. The integrated Prolog rule passed the
single-task test and the full regression on its first compile:
induction returns staple_gravity, the test output matches exactly,
and every previously solved task still passes. Induction is pure
verification: a cheap pre-filter (equal heights, a nonzero corner
indicator, an otherwise empty top row, an empty second row, the
indicator preserved in the output corner) followed by
transformation of every training pair.
